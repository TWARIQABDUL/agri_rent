Farming Equipment Rental Platform

Mission Introduction
Our project sits in agriculture, which is the base Rwanda's economy is built on. Farming
brings in about a third of the country's GDP and employs more people than any other sector,
and roughly three quarters of what the country grows comes from smallholder farmers [1],
[2]. Yet most of those farmers still prepare and harvest their land by hand or with old tools. A
tractor, an irrigation pump, or a harvester costs far more than an ordinary rural household can
put together.
AgriRent is a mobile app that links farmers who need equipment to the owners and
cooperatives who have it. Today a farmer chases equipment through phone calls and
neighbours and whatever they hear secondhand. With the app, that same farmer opens it, sees
verified equipment nearby, checks prices and ratings, and books it for the exact days they
need. We want to take the informal sharing that already happens, slow and patchy as it is, and
make it a clear local marketplace that runs on a basic smartphone.

This is worth fixing because two things ride on it: what a family earns, and whether it has
enough to eat. Miss a planting or harvesting window because no machine showed up in time,
and the yield falls, and that lost money is gone until the next season comes around. When
equipment is easy to find, price, and book, a farmer can move during the short window that
decides how the season goes. The idea also fits what Rwanda is already aiming for in Vision
2050 and the National Strategy for Transformation (NST2), both of which push farming away
from subsistence and toward commercial, tech-driven, increasingly digital work [1].

2. Literature Review

2.1 Problem Context

AgriRent deals with one main problem: African smallholder farmers have very little access to
machines, and no organised, trustworthy way to get hold of one when the work needs doing.
Most research agrees that mechanisation lifts yields, eases the labour burden, and gets
planting and harvesting done faster. The trouble is that it stays out of reach for most
smallholders, who make up the bulk of farmers in Rwanda and across the continent [3], [5].
So the cycle keeps turning. Farmers lean on manual labour, output stays low, and incomes
never get steady.

2.2 Evidence

The numbers behind the mechanisation gap are hard to argue with. The FAO puts Africa at
fewer than two tractors for every 1,000 hectares of cropland, while South Asia and Latin
America sit at about ten per 1,000 hectares [3]. One industry analysis sets the continent at
roughly eight tractors per 100 km² of arable land, well under the global average [6]. The
knock-on effect is that an estimated 65% of African smallholders still do the core jobs, land
preparation among them, by hand [5].

The squeeze comes from both sides of the market. Tractor owners often do not want to serve
places where the farms are small and spread out, since it costs a lot to reach them. And when
a machine is around, a smallholder with little bargaining power runs a real risk of getting
served late, or of depending on one of only a few owners [3], [4]. Money is the other wall. In
most African countries fewer than 10% of farmers can get a formal agricultural loan, so
buying a machine outright is not realistic for an ordinary household [6]. If you cannot own,
you need a dependable way to hire.
Digital and mobile tools have already shown they can close part of the gap. Rental and
sharing platforms help smallholders by keeping existing machines busier and putting owners
in touch with demand they would never have found by word of mouth [4]. The region has
live examples. Hello Tractor says it has served close to 200,000 smallholder farmers through
a digital tractor-sharing model and a fleet of several thousand machines [5]. Zoom out and the
pattern holds: digital agriculture solutions across low- and middle-income countries went
from about 152 in 2013 to around 1,400 in 2023, which says a lot about both the demand and
the scramble to meet it [7].

2.3 Gap Identification

Two gaps stand out. The first is range. A lot of the platforms that exist are built for tractors
and for bigger or commercial farms, which leaves out the irrigation pumps, sprayers,
harvesters, and small tools that smallholders actually reach for. The second is depth. The
count of digital agriculture solutions has shot up, but most stay small, and a common
complaint is that they brag about "registered farmers" instead of people who actually keep
using them. That usually means the tool is too fiddly, or it does not fit how a rural farmer
really works [7]. What is still missing is a simple, community-based platform that shows
clear local prices, real availability, and owner ratings for many kinds of equipment in one
place. AgriRent is built to be exactly that, taking the local sharing system that farmers already
trust but that wastes their time, and turning it into a clear mobile booking marketplace made
for low-resource, low-data settings.

3. Problem Statement and Hypothesis Statement

3.1 Problem Statement

Smallholder farmers in rural Rwanda grow much of the country's food, but they have no
structured, transparent, and timely way to find, rent, and book the equipment near them.
Owning a machine costs too much, and the fallback, asking around by word of mouth, leaves
them with unclear prices, no certainty about what is free, and no record they can use to judge
who to trust. Many end up back at manual labour and miss the planting and harvesting
windows that matter most [3], [5], [6].

3.2 Hypothesis Statement

Our claim is this: a simple, low-data mobile platform that connects farmers with nearby
owners and cooperatives, and that shows real availability, honest prices, and verified owner
ratings, will cut both the time and the guesswork involved in getting equipment, and will
raise the share of tasks that participating farmers finish on time. We expect this because
digital rental and sharing models have already been shown to widen smallholder access, by
keeping machines in use and linking owners to demand close by [4], [5].

Competitor Analysis
Hello Tractor is a direct competitor. It connects tractor owners with smallholder farmers
through a digital sharing system, and it uses GPS and fleet tools so owners can track their
machines and reach farmers who need them [9]. Its own site says the marketplace has served
over 2.5 million smallholder farmers and works with thousands of equipment owners, which
tells us the demand for digital equipment access is real [8].
TROTRO Tractor is another direct competitor. Farmers can request, schedule, and prepay
for tractor work from either a feature phone or a smartphone, which is close to what we are
doing, getting smallholders better access to machines over mobile [11]. It also lets owners
keep an eye on their tractors from a distance through GPS trackers, USSD, and apps [11].
EM3 AgriServices sits further from us, so we treat it as an indirect competitor. It also widens
access to costly equipment, but it runs as farming-as-a-service rather than a local rental
marketplace. Farmers pay per use for high-end technology, and EM3 backs them with an app,
call centres, and human reps, which helps people who are not always online [12].
Trringo is indirect too. It lets farmers use tractors and equipment without buying them, but it
works mostly in India, not Africa. It runs on a mobile app, a call centre, and a franchise
network that links farmers to machines on a pay-per-use basis. That is where our opening is:
we can build for African farmers and local owners, and lean on community renting in a way
Trringo does not.

Interview Summary
We drew on responses from more than ten potential users: small-scale farmers, equipment
owners, agriculture workers, one large-scale farmer, and one agriculture student. Most came
from farming areas like Bugesera, Muhanga, Musanze, Ruhango, Gasabo, Kabuga, Ntunga,
and Kigali.
Most of them find equipment informally. Seven ask neighbours or friends, five go through
local leaders or cooperatives, and four said they usually struggle to find it at all. The pattern
is hard to miss: there is no reliable way to see what is actually free to use.
The problems that came up most were equipment being hard to find nearby, high cost,
unclear prices, late availability, weak trust, and trouble with transport. Seven said they would
use a mobile app and three said maybe. So the demand is there, as long as the app stays
simple and easy to trust.

Unique Value Proposition (UVP)

AgriRent gives smallholder farmers a clear, community-based way to rent the equipment they
need, whether that is a tractor, an irrigation pump, a sprayer, a harvester, or a few hand tools.
Each listing shows the local price, what is available right now, and a rating for the owner, and
the whole thing runs in a light app that works on a basic phone and a weak connection. Word
of mouth and the tractor-only platforms cannot do this. What AgriRent does is take the local
sharing that farmers already trust and turn it into a quick, reliable booking system, so a farmer
can lock in equipment during the narrow window that makes or breaks a season.

Reframed Problem Statement

A smallholder farmer in rural Rwanda usually cannot afford to own machinery, so when the
work comes due she has to hire it, and the only way to do that is to ask around. That informal

route leaves her guessing about the price, unsure whether the equipment is even free, and
with nothing to fall back on if the owner does not show. The gear then turns up late, or it
never comes. She misses the planting or harvesting window, goes back to doing the work by
hand, and earns less for the season. Meanwhile the owner's machine sits in a shed because he
has no dependable way to hear that someone nearby needs it.

User Flow

the steps are summarised below.

Farmer — Search and Rent Flow (happy path and edge cases)

Reflection: Linking Design Decisions to Research
The clearest theme in the interviews and the journey map was timing. Farmers cannot count
on finding equipment when they actually need it, and because nobody is sure what a piece
should cost or whether the other party will deliver, word of mouth turns into a slow, stressful
chase. That is what pushed us toward a plain dashboard with equipment sorted into obvious
categories and a search that works at the village level and shows what is free right now. A
farmer can book inside the short planting window instead of spending days on the phone.

The competitor analysis pointed to the same hole every time. Hello Tractor and TROTRO
Tractor do their job well, but they are built around tractors. EM3 runs everything through a
central service. Word of mouth keeps no records at all. Not one of them gives a farmer a
simple local marketplace that covers different kinds of equipment and shows honest prices
and ratings. So we made two calls. We cover tractors, pumps, sprayers, harvesters, and small
hand tools instead of tractors alone, and we put the price, the distance, and the owner's rating
on every listing, so the trust that used to come from knowing a neighbour now comes from
the listing itself.

The empathy maps brought out the owner's half of this. Their machines sit idle, and they
worry about getting paid and about how far the gear has to travel. That is the reason for the
owner dashboard, the booking confirmation, and the ratings system: owners get steady
demand that pays, plus a reputation worth looking after. One more thing shaped everything.
Rural users are on weak connections and basic phones, so keeping the app simple and light
on data is not a nice extra but a requirement, and it is what drives the Choice Principle layout
and the lightweight build across the prototype.
